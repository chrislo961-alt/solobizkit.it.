Cash flow basics for small businesses

A business can be profitable on paper and still run short of cash. The timing of money in and money out matters just as much as the amount.

Profit is not cash

Profit measures revenue minus expenses over a period. Cash flow tracks when money actually enters or leaves the bank. If you invoice today with 30-day terms, the sale may count as revenue before the cash arrives.

Why small businesses get squeezed

  • Customers pay later than expected
  • Large expenses are due before client payments arrive
  • Taxes or VAT were not reserved
  • Too much money is tied up in stock or upfront costs
  • Recurring subscriptions quietly increase
  • Growth requires spending before new revenue is collected

Track the next 30–90 days

List expected incoming payments by date, then list rent, software, suppliers, payroll, tax, loan payments and other commitments. Use conservative dates for uncertain customer payments rather than assuming every invoice arrives on time.

A cash forecast does not need to be sophisticated. A simple date-by-date view of expected money in and out can reveal problems early enough to act.

Improve cash flow without increasing sales

Send invoices promptly, reduce unnecessary payment delays, collect deposits when appropriate, follow up overdue invoices consistently and remove recurring costs that no longer create value. Better timing can improve liquidity even when revenue is unchanged.

Keep a buffer

A cash reserve gives the business time when a large client pays late or an unexpected expense appears. The right amount depends on the stability of your revenue and fixed commitments.

A simple monthly habit

At least once a month, review current cash, invoices due, overdue amounts and major commitments for the next 30–90 days. Cash problems are much easier to solve when you see them before the account balance is already low.
