Cash flow basics for small businesses

Profit tells you whether the business makes money. Cash flow tells you whether the money arrives in time to pay what is due.

Start with a 30–90 day cash view

Do not begin with a complicated model. List opening cash, expected incoming payments by date and expected outgoing payments by date.

Use conservative payment dates

If a customer invoice is due on the 10th but that customer often pays a week late, a conservative forecast should reflect the likely timing, not the most optimistic date.

A cash forecast is useful because it can be wrong early. If you see a possible shortage three weeks ahead, you still have options.

Separate committed, likely and uncertain income

Committed

Invoice already sent or contractually scheduled payment.

Likely

Work agreed but not yet invoiced.

Uncertain

Proposal, lead or hoped-for sale. Do not treat it as cash in the bank.

Committed outflow

Rent, payroll, tax, supplier bills, subscriptions and loan payments.

Actions that can improve timing

  • Invoice immediately when milestones are reached
  • Use deposits or staged billing where appropriate
  • Reduce unnecessarily long payment terms
  • Follow up overdue invoices consistently
  • Schedule major purchases against actual cash timing
  • Cancel recurring costs that no longer create value

A monthly cash routine

Update opening cash, unpaid invoices, committed expenses and the next 30–90 days. Then compare last month's forecast with what actually happened so your assumptions improve over time.
